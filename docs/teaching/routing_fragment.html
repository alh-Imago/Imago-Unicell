Cells sit on a flat grid and words travel between neighbours. What happens when two paths need to cross? Two wires on a flat board cannot, so there are two small wiring tiles. The cross tile lets one word go straight through while another crosses it at right angles. The corner tile turns words round a bend. Neither one changes a word. They only decide which way it leaves.

1. One tile, four faces

A tile has four faces, N, S, E and W, and each face can be an entry and an exit. A word that comes in by a face is held in that face's own slot and offered to the paired face on the other side. The four slots are independent, so four words can be inside at once, each going its own way. Crossing a tile costs one tick.

  • Cross: N and S are paired, and E and W are paired. A word entering at W leaves at E, one entering at N leaves at S, and so on, both ways.
  • Corner, turn 0: E is paired with N, and W is paired with S.
  • Corner, turn 1: E is paired with S, and W is paired with N. This is the mirror image, so a corner can send "E to N while W goes to S" or "E to S while W goes to N".

2. What to notice

  • Crossing needs no decisions. The cross tile never looks at a word. Pairs of faces are wired together, and that is the whole job. Without it, one path would have to wait for the other, or take a long detour.
  • Four slots mean no collisions. A word going W to E and a word going N to S use different slots and pass each other in the same tick. Only two words entering by the same face have to queue.
  • A stuck exit holds up only its own lane. Switch one exit to stuck: the word aimed at it waits where it is, and the other three lanes carry on. This is the same waiting rule as every other cell.
  • A corner is a cross with a bend. Both have the same four slots and the same one-tick cost. The only difference is which face each slot leads to. A corner's turn setting picks one of the two bend patterns.
  • They are routing only. The composer's routes are built from plain relay cells and these tiles. A word that goes through ten tiles has taken ten ticks, and the words that came in had their order kept.

A simplified picture, one tick at a time. A word that arrives in a tick is held; it is offered at the next tick, and leaves when the neighbour on that side accepts it (the "accepting" switches stand for that neighbour).
