Cells only talk to their immediate neighbours, so a design is not finished when every operation has a cell: the cells must also be put on squares and joined by relay cells so that values travel from where they are made to where they are used. That job is the placer. Making the result small and still correct is the tightener. This page shows both on real compiles: two placers side by side, tightening by spacing, folding into bands, and the "rat's nest" way of shrinking one connection step by step.

1. Why placing is hard

  • A value moves one square per tick, and only to a neighbour. Two cells far apart need a chain of relay cells between them, and each relay is a cell you pay for.
  • No diagonals, and a relay can only go straight (the router's own notes record this). A route can turn only where a cell is set to turn it.
  • Routes must not cross. Two chains that meet on one square would be one chain. There is a cross tile (see the Routing page) but the placers described here do not use it, so a design that cannot be drawn without crossing is refused loudly rather than miscompiled.
  • A cell has four faces. A producer has three output faces, so one value can feed at most three consumers.

2. Two placers, side by side

The compiler has two placers. The growth placer builds the design outward from where the previous operation ended, adding a few padding cells before the next shape. The routed placer first arranges the operations in columns on a virtual canvas, then routes every connection across the whole canvas at once, and only then turns it into cells. 3. Tightening by spacing

The routed placer spaces its operations apart on the canvas by a number, the spacing. Wide spacing makes routing easy and the design large; narrow spacing makes it small and may make routing fail. 5. The rat's nest: loose first, then shrink

An older and simpler idea, from Alan: place things loosely with plenty of room so it is easy to get right, see where routes meet (the nexus points, such as a priority cell where two values converge), then pull each connection in toward its nexus one step at a time, checking after every step that nothing broke, and stop the moment a step would. 6. What the placers have taught us

The flex side has its own layout engine and the project keeps a file of what each design taught it. A few of those lessons, in plain words (the file has all of them):

  • Make a subtract order-free. A subtract is the common two-operand operation where operand order matters, and when the minuend's route is the long one the balancer cannot fix it. Rewriting a - b as a + (-1) * b removes the problem at the cost of a multiplier. The file marks this rule as done by hand in one engine and not yet automatic in the placer.
  • Route the shortest, most constrained lane first, while both ends are still open.
  • Leave room at the doors. Pin entries and exits to the edge of a tile and put a one-relay stub on each open face before routing, or earlier lanes wall the pin in.
  • A cell that makes two words is two lanes. A carry or high word leaving by its own faces has to be laid like a second output.
  • Retry with seeds. A placement that cannot route is undone and tried again in a different order.

7. Limits, stated by the code itself

  • Routing is a sequence of heuristics, not an optimum. Shortest-first, then seeded reorderings, then more spacing. The source notes that routing under non-crossing constraints is a hard problem in general, which is why the Composer exists as a human fallback.
  • A design that cannot be laid out without crossings fails loudly. It does not produce a wrong answer.
  • The default tightener does not try every spacing. It tries 3, 4, 5, 6, 8 and 12 in that order and keeps the first that routes. In our run, asking for spacing 2 directly gave a smaller layout (18 cells in 5 by 6, against 20 in 7 by 8) that also gave the right answer for the inputs we tried. We did not run it further, so we cannot say 2 is always safe.
  • The rat's-nest tightener is narrow. It shrinks a free-moving value toward a fixed target. Its source says shrinking a connection between two fixed meeting points, where moving one end means re-routing everything on it, was a harder problem addressed later (there is a separate routine for a combine unit), and we have not shown that one here.
  • It is tested on small designs. The test for the rat's nest says a four-input reduction tree went from 186 loose cells to 90 and still computed correctly; that is quoted from the test file's description and was not re-run.
