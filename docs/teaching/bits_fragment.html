Computers work on whole words of bits at a time, and two small cells show how a word can be reshaped. The shift stage slides every bit left or right by a fixed amount. The mask cell switches whole groups of bits off. Together they can pull a piece out of a word, which is most of what unpacking data means.

1. Build a word, shift it, mask it

The word is 18 bits wide, like the Tang Nano 20K build. Type a number, choose a shift, and click groups of the mask to switch them off. Each stage's bits are drawn so you can see exactly what happened.

2. What to notice

  • A left shift is a multiply, until bits fall off. Sliding left by 3 multiplies by 8, as long as nothing that matters is pushed past the top. This is the cheap multiply by a power of two: no multiplier cell is needed, only wiring.
  • A right shift is a divide, with the remainder thrown away. The bits that fall off the right are gone for good.
  • This shift does not know about signs. It fills with zeros, so a negative number shifted right comes out large and positive. Keeping the sign needs a different shift.
  • The shift amount is fixed when the design is built. That is why the cell is so small: there is nothing to choose at run time. To shift by more, chain several stages.
  • The mask works on groups, not single bits. The mask word has 8 bits; at 18 bits wide each mask bit covers a group of 3 data bits, which gives six groups. Setting a mask bit sets its whole group to zero.
  • Shift then mask pulls a field out. Slide the field you want down to the bottom, then switch off the groups above it. Try the last example.
